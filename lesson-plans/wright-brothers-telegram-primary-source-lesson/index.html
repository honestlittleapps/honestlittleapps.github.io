Reading a Primary Source: The Wright Brothers' Telegram: free lesson plan

A ready-to-teach 45-minute lesson with objectives, a step-by-step plan, a printable worksheet and exit tickets. Free, no sign-up.

Worksheet + exit tickets (PDF)

Objectives

  • Read a real 1903 telegram and identify who wrote it, when and why.
  • Spot errors in a primary source and explain how historians check them.

Supports reading informational text and historical thinking: sourcing, close reading and corroboration.

Materials

  • The telegram text (on the worksheet)
  • Pencils and highlighters

Lesson steps

  1. Hook (5 min). Ask: 'If you'd just done something no one had ever done, how would you tell your family in 1903, with no phones?' Introduce telegrams: paid by the word, so people wrote very short.
  2. Read the source (10 min). Read the telegram aloud: “Success four flights thursday morning all against twenty one mile wind started from Level with engine power alone average speed through air thirty one miles longest 57 seconds inform Press home Christmas. Orevelle Wright”. Students highlight who, what, when and where.
  3. Detective work (15 min). Tell students the telegram has two mistakes made in sending it: the longest flight was really 59 seconds, and Orville's name is misspelled. Discuss: how would a historian find out? (Diaries, photos, other witnesses.)
  4. Rewrite it (10 min). Students rewrite the telegram as a modern text message in 25 words or fewer, keeping every fact.
  5. Exit ticket (5 min). Name one thing a primary source can tell us that a textbook can't, and one reason to double-check it.

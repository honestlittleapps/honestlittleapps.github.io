Read the Bible in Context: a Free 5-Day Course

One short chapter and one study skill a day. By Friday you will have read the whole book of Ruth and know how to read any book of the Bible the same way. 5 lessons · about 15 minutes each · free, no sign-up.

Get a reminder each day: Works with Apple, Google and Outlook calendars. Starts tomorrow.

What you’ll do

  1. Read the whole story first
  2. Follow the people and their names
  3. Learn the culture behind the scene
  4. See how the ending connects to the bigger story
  5. Apply it without skipping the steps
Day 1 · Ruth 1

Read the whole story first

Most confusion about a Bible verse comes from reading it alone. Context starts with the big picture: who wrote to whom, when, and what kind of writing it is.

  1. Read Ruth 1 straight through without stopping to study.
  2. Notice the first verse: “in the days when the judges ruled” (Ruth 1:1). That places the story in a dark, unstable time in Israel’s history; the book of Judges ends with “every man did that which was right in his own eyes.”
  3. Ask: what kind of writing is this? Ruth is a short story (narrative), so it teaches by what happens, not by giving commands.

Today: Write one sentence: what is chapter 1 about?

Day 2 · Ruth 2

Follow the people and their names

In Hebrew stories, names and character changes carry the meaning.

  1. Read Ruth 2. List the characters: Naomi, Ruth, Boaz, the harvesters.
  2. Look back at Ruth 1:20: Naomi asks to be called Mara. Naomi sounds like “pleasant”; Mara means “bitter.” Watch how her mood changes in 2:20.
  3. Notice the word kindness. Boaz shows generosity beyond what the law required (2:15–16). The Hebrew idea behind it, hesed, means loyal, covenant love and runs through the whole book.

Today: Circle every act of kindness in chapter 2. Who shows it, and to whom?

Day 3 · Ruth 3

Learn the culture behind the scene

Some scenes only make sense once you know the customs of the time.

  1. Read Ruth 3. It can feel strange to modern readers.
  2. Look up two passages: Leviticus 25:25 (a relative could “redeem” family land) and Deuteronomy 25:5–6 (caring for a widow so a family line continues). Boaz is a kinsman-redeemer, a close relative with the right and duty to help.
  3. Ask what Naomi’s plan means in that culture: Ruth is asking Boaz to take on that role publicly and honorably.

Today: Write down one thing that changed about how you read chapter 3 after the background.

Day 4 · Ruth 4

See how the ending connects to the bigger story

Context includes where a book sits in the whole Bible.

  1. Read Ruth 4. Watch the legal scene at the city gate (4:1–10).
  2. Read the last verses (4:17–22). Ruth’s son Obed becomes the grandfather of King David.
  3. Turn to Matthew 1:5. Ruth, a Moabite outsider, is named in the family line of Jesus. A small family story turns out to be part of a much bigger one.

Today: Draw a simple line: Ruth → Obed → Jesse → David → … → Jesus.

Day 5 · Ruth 1–4

Apply it without skipping the steps

Application is the last step, not the first. Now that you know what it meant then, you can ask what it means now.

  1. Ask three questions about the whole book: What did it mean to the first readers? What does it show about God? What does it ask of me?
  2. Notice that God is rarely mentioned doing anything dramatic in Ruth. He works through ordinary faithfulness: gleaning, kindness and keeping promises.
  3. Choose one practical act of loyal kindness (hesed) you can do this week.

Today: Use the same five steps on your next book: whole story, people, culture, bigger story, then application.

Questions

How long is the book of Ruth?

Four chapters. Each takes about five minutes to read.

Do I need a study Bible?

No. Any translation works. Look up the cross-references given in each lesson.
